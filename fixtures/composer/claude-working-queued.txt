  for each in memory, and repainted whichever one you were looking at.

  Its defining feature was detaching. You could disconnect — deliberately, or because your modem dropped — and reattach later with processes still running and output intact. That one capability
  changed remote work: long compiles, IRC clients, and debuggers survived flaky links. Screen also set conventions that outlived it: a prefix key (^A), numbered windows, a copy-scrollback mode,
  and multiuser session sharing.

  dtach and the minimalist line

  Not everyone wanted the whole apparatus. Ned T. Crigler's dtach (2004) does exactly one thing Screen does — attach and detach — and nothing else. No windows, no panes, no status line, no copy
  mode. It allocates a pty, keeps a small buffer of recent output to redraw on reattach, and otherwise passes bytes straight through, in well under a thousand lines of C.

  That has a real payoff. Screen and tmux both parse and re-render terminal output, which historically broke full-screen and 256-color applications in subtle ways; dtach emulates nothing, so
  programs behave as they would on a bare terminal. The cost is no session sharing, no splitting, and no history beyond the buffer. Users who want both halves compose them: abduco (a dtach-like
  detacher) paired with Marc André Tanner's dvtm for tiling.

  tmux and the modern era

  Screen's codebase aged badly, carrying decades of portability workarounds. In 2007 Nicholas Marriott released tmux under a BSD license, structured as client–server: a background server owns the
  sessions, and thin clients attach over a Unix socket. That split made scripting natural — tmux send-keys, tmux new-window — and tmux treated tiled panes as first-class rather than bolted on.
  OpenBSD replaced Screen with tmux in its base system in 2009.

  Byobu wrapped Screen and later tmux in a friendlier status line. Zellij (2021, Rust) added discoverable keybindings, layouts, and WebAssembly plugins for users who found tmux's defaults hostile.
  Meanwhile iTerm2, WezTerm, and Kitty absorbed tabs and splits into the emulator itself. What they cannot do is survive the loss of the connection — which is why, on any remote host, the
  multiplexer is still the first tool people reach for.
